# Ecommerce & Product Experience

Commerce UX helps a person understand an offer, compare alternatives, make a confident choice, and
know what happens after committing. The objective is informed progress, not a larger conversion
number at any cost.

## Product decision model

Before changing a product page or checkout, identify:

- what is being bought, by whom, for which job;
- what varies by option, quantity, weight, time, location, stock, service level, or eligibility;
- which facts are needed before the decision and which can wait;
- what uncertainty, cost, delivery condition, return term, or consequence could surprise the person;
- what happens in cart, payment, fulfillment, cancellation, support, and post-purchase use.

Use real and variable content. A product page is a system: catalog data, availability, pricing,
quantity, cart, payment, fulfillment, support, and account state must agree.

## Variable quantity and honest totals

For products sold by weight, length, time, or another variable unit, distinguish visibly between:

- the unit and its price;
- the selected or estimated quantity;
- the estimated subtotal or total and how it can change;
- minimums, maximums, rounding, availability, fees, delivery, and the point at which the amount is
  confirmed.

Do not use a preset, slider, sticky action, or compressed summary if it hides uncertainty or impairs
comparison. Offer common selections only when they reflect real behavior, retain manual or precise
input where needed, and explain the consequence before commitment.

## Trust at the decision point

Place relevant proof and terms near the claim or action. Make the following discoverable without a
search through the interface:

- who supplies the product or service;
- price, unit, quantity, fees, taxes, delivery, timing, and billing start;
- stock or availability only when real;
- returns, cancellation, support, privacy, and data use;
- what is reversible and what is not.

Never hide cost, preselect an add-on, fabricate scarcity, or make cancellation harder than purchase.

## Mobile behavior

On a small viewport, test content order, zoom/dynamic type, focus, keyboard, safe areas, sticky
actions, product comparison, and recovery after interruption. A persistent CTA is justified only if
it remains understandable, does not cover essential content, and works with keyboard and assistive
technology. These are validation questions, not visual rules.

## Validation

Measure whether representative people can find, understand, compare, choose, correct, and complete:

- comprehension of unit, quantity, total, terms, and next consequence;
- task success, error, abandonment, time, confidence, and support need;
- differences by device, input method, risk, accessibility need, and user stage;
- downstream surprises, cancellation, refund, complaint, and trust signals.

An observed conversion change does not prove that the design improved usability or trust. Record the
user outcome and business outcome separately.

## Source note

The mobile decision patterns in this reference are adapted from the user-provided synthesis associated
with [“Make Any UI design Look More Senior”](https://youtu.be/GGg61sdEjeI?si=nXdomsGfVsfp7LFF).
The video metadata was verified, but its full content was not directly retrieved during authoring;
the patterns above therefore remain hypotheses to validate, not empirical claims from the video.

[Baymard product-page research](https://baymard.com/research/product-page) is useful directional
ecommerce evidence, not a universal prescription for every catalog, market, or device.
